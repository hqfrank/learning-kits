NVIDIA GPU anatomy · story 1

A GPU hides waiting by switching workers — and runs fast only while its parts stay close.A GPU hides latency by switching warps — and is fast only while the working set stays near.

Picture the whole GPU as one city of workshops. Each city block is one SM; inside it, benches of 32 workers move in lockstep; a foreman hands the next job only to a bench that is ready. Parts come from a shelf, a storeroom, a shared warehouse, or a far port — and how far is how slow. Flip to Engineer view for the real names.

1One job card, thirty-two workers

A bench is 32 workers who all do the same job at the same time — because the foreman hands out one card for the whole bench. Step through.

2Four foremen per block — the first ceiling

A city block (one SM) has four workshop bays. Each bay has one foreman who can start one card per tick. That caps the block at four — no matter how many workers stand ready.

3Waiting is hidden by switching, not by guessing

When a bench sends a runner to fetch a part, it must wait for the round trip. A CPU would guess ahead to stay busy. A GPU does something simpler: the foreman just switches to another ready bench.

A runner's round trip takes 12 ticks. The foreman wants to start a card every single tick and never go idle. Besides the waiting bench, how many other ready benches does the bay need?

4The memory ladder is a map — distance is delay

Where a part lives decides the round trip. The shelf is at the bench; the storeroom is a short walk; the warehouse is across the block; the port is across town. Pick a place and a board, and watch the runner.

The ladder, and the two numbers that matterLatency rises and capacity rises as you descend; bandwidth falls. The kit stresses two facts: L2-resident reads are 12.4× (Orin) / 14.6× (Thor) faster than DRAM, and L2 latency barely improved Orin→Thor (146→156 ns) — only its capacity grew 8× (05).

5The cliff: when the hot data outgrows the warehouse

The warehouse (L2) is one fixed size for the whole city. While your hot data fits inside it, every runner's trip is short. One byte over, and every trip goes to the far port.

A model's hot data is 30 MB. It fits Thor's 32 MB warehouse, but not Orin's 4 MB. For a streaming read, how much slower is it on Orin?

6Reuse is keeping the part on the bench

If distance is delay, the fix is obvious: fetch a part once, then use it many times before sending it back. In GPU terms that is tiling — and it is the single biggest lever a kernel has.

Where the city metaphor breaks

  • A GPU is 16–20 blocks, not a sprawling city; the two boards here differ mostly in how many blocks and how big the shared warehouse is, not in the per-block layout.
  • Workers on one bench are only in lockstep until they branch; on divergence the hardware runs each path with the other lanes masked off, then reconverges.
  • Crews (blocks) run in any order and cannot wait on each other; only workers within a bench, or a crew at its own barrier, are coordinated.
  • The shelf (registers) is not a cache: parts are assigned to a worker at hiring, never searched for by address — so a working set never "fits on the shelf" the way it fits in the warehouse.
  • The far port (DRAM) is shared with the CPU city on these integrated chips: a busy CPU can slow the GPU's runners even when the GPU has compute to spare.
