1The logbook tears out its own pages

The logbook has a fixed number of pages. Watch what happens to the page that shows the fault. Step through.

2Three cars, or a fleet change?

Every car sends its lights, and they are cheap, therefore they choose which logbooks to read. But a share from a small family of cars is noisy.

Last week no car showed a rare light. Today 3 % of a {{small_family_devices}}-car family shows it, and 3 % of a {{large_family_devices}}-car family. Which is more likely a real change?

The modelThe share of cars with the light on is a sample proportion, p̂ = lit cars ÷ active cars. Its standard error is √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n). Halve n and the error grows by √2. That is why the trend test in scene 3 weighs each day by its cars (A2).

3Is the light getting worse?

One light, one family, eight weeks. The question is a slope. But some days rest on many cars and some on very few.

4Every line was printed by a rubber stamp

The lights picked the cars, therefore the mechanic now opens their logbooks. Millions of different lines turn out to come from a few hundred stamps.

5Tally each page, then play twenty questions

Nobody labelled which pages are faults, therefore the method learns what normal pages look like and looks for the page that is easiest to single out.

The forest gives the odd page a score of {{iforest_score_3_splits_psi256}}. What does {{iforest_score_3_splits_psi256}} mean?

The formulas = 2−E(h)/c(ψ), where E(h) is the average number of questions over the forest and c(ψ) is the average for a typical point among ψ = {{iforest_subsample_size_default}}: c = {{iforest_c_256}}. At E(h) = c the score is {{iforest_score_at_average_path}}. Which scores raise an alarm is a threshold you set (B3).

6Where did the car leave its usual route?

A score says which page is odd. But a tally has no order, so it cannot say what happened. The order of the stamps can.

7The road, not the car

The method found odd pages and named where they left the route. But is an odd page a broken car?

An illustrative pilot turns {{pilot_tickets}} odd logbooks into work orders. How many of them were the road, not the car?

The rule: before a work order, get a second, independent signal. A light and an odd page, or an odd page and other cars on other roads doing the same. The mechanic still decides; the method removes the search, not the judgement. C4

And how good is the detector?

Test it on cars whose fault you already know. A hand-built set is small, therefore report the rate with its range.

Why Wilson, and why false alarms tooThe simple normal interval misbehaves near 0 % and 100 % and with few cases; the Wilson interval does not. A detector that flags everything catches 100 %, so the detection rate means nothing without the false-alarm rate next to it (C1).

Where the garage breaks

  • No person writes the logbook: dozens of software components print into it at once, at hundreds of thousands of lines a day.
  • Nobody sees the warning light. A metric is a record sent to the cloud when an event happens; a car with no event sends nothing, and a car that stopped reporting looks the same (C3).
  • "The road" is each household's own network, not a road that many cars share. Cars "on other roads" means devices on other access points.
  • The twenty questions are random and blind, not clever, and the forest averages {{iforest_trees_default}} such games.
  • The mechanic never gets the car, only an uploaded copy of the book, and only if it was uploaded before the pages were torn out.
