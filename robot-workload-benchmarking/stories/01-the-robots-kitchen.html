2More guests, more plating

Each camera frame, the kitchen finds everyone in view. Some work is paid once per frame; some is paid once per guest.

Each extra guest adds work for the oven and for the cooks. Which grows faster?

The modeloven ms per frame = fixed + N × per-guest; cook ms per frame = (fixed + N × per-guest) ÷ cores allowed. Per-frame: detection and NMS once on the whole picture. Per-guest: face, re-ID, pose on the oven; Kalman, Hungarian matching, depth sampling and One Euro smoothing on the cooks (A2).

3The time-card trap

A container gets a time card: so much cook-time per period. When the card runs out, every burner it uses stops until the next period. The counter that records this is the most misread number on the bench.

Four burners stood frozen for 50 ms of wall time. How much does throttled_usec add for this period?

4Zero late plates. Is that proof?

Late walking plates are rare events. Counting zero of them tells you less than it feels like.

You watched for 2 minutes. The walking plate never missed its bell. What is the honest claim?

Why Poisson, and why blocksMisses are rare and roughly independent, so their count is Poisson. The exact interval comes from the count alone. Consecutive cycles of a running robot are correlated, so intervals on percentiles or means need block methods (moving-block bootstrap, or a t-interval across replicate blocks), not ÷√N (B3).

Where the kitchen breaks

  • A real scheduler does not give orders to a named cook: any free core runs any runnable thread.
  • A time card belongs to a container, not to one cook; it is spent by all of that container's threads together.
  • The oven is not one oven: a GPU runs thousands of threads in groups of 32 on many SMs. See GPU anatomy.
  • The pantry door is shared because CPU and GPU share one memory on an integrated SoC; on a desktop with a separate graphics card they do not.
