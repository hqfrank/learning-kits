A moving body changes the shape of the CSI across subcarriers, which the single RSSI number throws away, and wider channels keep more of that shape

A synthetic room with one Wi-Fi link: a direct path, wall reflections up to second order and one reflector you can move (a person). The receiver's channel estimate H(f) is the sum of those paths, each rotated by its delay (note A1). Move the reflector or walk it along a short path. RSSI, the power summed over the channel, moves by one number. The per-subcarrier amplitude (CSI) also changes shape: some subcarriers rise while others fall. That shape is the extra information. At 20 MHz in a small room it is modest. At 80 MHz, where more paths are resolved, it is larger than the RSSI change. The Fresnel ellipse, Doppler and report-rate panel show why placement and sampling rate matter.

Synthetic channel, not a measurement. Room size, wall and body reflectivity are page assumptions assumption. Subcarrier counts, spacing and wavelengths are public 802.11 values loaded from constants.json. Body blocking and diffraction are not modelled.
